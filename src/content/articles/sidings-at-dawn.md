I went to the marshalling yard to photograph the light, and came back having photographed a
queueing system.

A siding is a length of track that goes nowhere on purpose. Its entire job is to hold rolling
stock off the main line so that faster, more urgent traffic can pass — a buffer, a parking lot, a
place to wait that doesn't block anyone behind you. Stand at the throat of a yard at six in the
morning, with the mist still sitting on the rails, and you are looking at backpressure made of
steel.

## What the camera kept finding

I had gone for the obvious pictures: long lines of converging rails, the geometry of points and
crossings, a signal gantry against a paling sky. What I kept actually pressing the shutter for was
the *waiting*. A single wagon parked alone on a dead-end spur. Couplings slack because nothing was
pulling. A yard that was busy precisely by standing still.

It is the same thing I'd spent the winter chasing in software — the realisation, while
[optimising a database write path](/articles/optimizing-database-inserts/), that the expensive part
is never the work but the waiting, and that a good system is one that lets things wait *cheaply*.
The Victorians solved this with gravity humps and slack track a century before we gave it a name
and a Greek letter.

There's something steadying about that. The abstractions we treat as new and difficult have
physical ancestors you can walk up to and touch, cold and rusting and entirely unbothered by our
dashboards. I think that's why I keep [taking the long way round](/articles/the-long-way-round/) —
the detour is where you meet the older version of the idea.

A slow tool is not a tool that works slowly. It is a tool that is slow to *change* — slow to update
itself, slow to demand your attention, slow to go out of date, slow to need replacing. A cast-iron
pan is a slow tool. A plain text file is a slow tool. A bicycle is a slow tool. None of them is slow
to use; all of them are slow to betray you.

We have spent two decades optimising the wrong axis. The industry got very good at making tools
fast to *acquire* — instant to install, free to start, frictionless to onboard — and in doing so
made them fast to *decay*. The app that updated its interface overnight. The service that was
discontinued. The format that no longer opens. Speed of acquisition turns out to trade directly
against durability, and durability is the only kind of speed that compounds.

## The test of a slow tool

Ask of any tool: *will this still work, unchanged, in ten years, without anyone's permission?* A
pencil passes. A paper map passes. The [single-station radio I built](/articles/paper-radio/) was an
attempt to make a modern object pass, by stripping out everything in it that existed to serve a
business model rather than me.

Most software fails the test, and not for technical reasons. It fails because someone needs it to
keep changing in order to keep being paid. The complexity this adds is the [accidental
kind](/articles/on-complexity/) — difficulty introduced for reasons that have nothing to do with
your problem.

## Choosing slow

I'm not a purist about it. I write this on a fast computer, with fast tools, on a fast network.
But where I can, for the things I do every day, I now reach deliberately for the slow option: the
text file over the app, the script over the service, the object over the subscription. Not out of
nostalgia. Out of a wish to own my tools again — to use things that are finished, that ask nothing
of me, and that will be exactly as useful tomorrow as they were the day I picked them up.

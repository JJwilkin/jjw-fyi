You can fly from here to the far edge of the continent in two hours and arrive having experienced
nothing but two airports and the gap between them. I took six days and four trains instead, and I
would do it again tomorrow.

The case for slow travel is usually made on romantic grounds — the changing landscape, the dining
car, the strangers — and all of that is true and worth having. But I want to make the duller,
sturdier case, which is about *resolution*. A flight samples your journey at two points. A train
samples it continuously. You feel the country change under you: the language on the platform
signs, the crops in the fields, the gauge of the track, the way the light goes from northern and
thin to southern and heavy. The distance becomes real because you paid for it in hours.

## A map I keep redrawing

I travel with a paper map and a pencil, and I draw the route as I go — not for navigation, the
phone does that, but because drawing a line makes you *attend* to it. Where did we cross the river?
Which range were those mountains? The pencil is a forcing function for noticing, the same way a
[notebook is for the swifts](/articles/field-note-the-first-swift/) and a camera was for the
[railway sidings](/articles/sidings-at-dawn/).

There were sidings everywhere on this trip, as it happens — every border, every junction, a yard
full of waiting stock. I'd spent the spring thinking about queues and [walking to understand
them](/articles/a-walk-to-clear-the-cache/), and here was a whole continent plumbed with the
physical version, humming along quite happily at a utilisation no software system would survive.

The long way round is never the efficient route. That's the entire point. Efficiency is for things
you've already decided don't deserve your attention, and I'm increasingly unwilling to decide that
about very much.

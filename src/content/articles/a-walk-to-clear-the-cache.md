The bug had me for three days. I knew the shape of it — a tail latency that came and went with no
pattern I could hold — and I had the dashboards memorised to the point of uselessness. On the
fourth morning I gave up and went for a walk, which is to say I did the most productive thing
available and felt guilty about it the entire time.

The route is the same one I always take when I'm stuck: out past the allotments, along the brook,
up onto the ridge where the wind takes the top off your thoughts. Forty minutes out, forty back. By
the brook I wasn't thinking about the bug. By the ridge the answer was simply *there*, fully formed,
the way these things arrive — it was a queue sitting in the red, and I'd been [reading the wrong
metric](/articles/the-shape-of-a-queue/) the whole time.

## Why it works, as far as I can tell

I don't think walking makes you cleverer. I think it does two duller things. First, it removes the
dashboard — the fixation, the worn groove of attention that keeps you re-running the same dead
query. Second, it puts your body into a steady rhythm that the mind can run underneath, the way a
background process gets cycles when the foreground finally lets go. The cache, so to speak, gets a
chance to clear.

There is good company in this. Nietzsche: "all truly great thoughts are conceived while walking."
Less grandly, every engineer I trust has a version of the walk — a shower, a drive, a washing-up.
The common thread is that none of them involve the screen, and all of them involve the body doing
something rhythmic and undemanding.

What the walk also does, which the desk never does, is put me back among things that have nothing
to do with the problem. A heron standing in the brook with infinite patience. The [first swift of
the year](/articles/field-note-the-first-swift/), back from Africa, screaming over the rooftops. It
is hard to keep believing your tail-latency bug is the most important thing in the universe while a
bird that weighs less than a pencil has just flown from another continent to nest in next door's
eaves. The proportion is restored, and with it — somehow — the answer.

This is not an argument I can put in a runbook. But it belongs in the same family as everything I
believe about [legible complexity](/articles/on-complexity/): the mind, like a system, has a
utilisation past which it only gets slower. The walk is how I keep mine off the cliff.
